## Purpose

Use this procedure before committing to a plan to make plausible failure visible while the plan can still change. It asks the team to imagine that the chosen plan has failed, generate reasons independently, group them without blaming people, and turn the most material paths into leading signals, mitigations, and stop conditions. The result is a more cautious plan with explicit residual uncertainty, not a prediction that failure will occur.

## Use when

Use it when a concrete plan, launch, migration, hire, policy, or other proposed action exists and optimism, conformity, or deadline pressure could hide risk. It is useful for cross-functional work with operational, market, technical, organizational, or external dependencies. Use a short future date and a defined failure meaning. A premortem can use sparse evidence, but its imagined reasons must be labelled hypotheses and connected to checks or actions.

## Do not use when

Do not use it when no plan or proposed action exists, when an immediate incident requires response rather than speculation, or when the exercise would replace a required safety, legal, or technical review. Do not treat the most vivid story as the most likely failure. Do not turn a group’s anxiety into a numeric probability without a defensible reference class. If the plan is already binding and cannot be changed, use a risk or incident procedure and state the constraint.

## Inputs to establish

State the chosen plan, intended outcome, commitment date, affected people or systems, and what counts as failure. Set the time horizon for the imagined retrospective and collect known dependencies, assumptions, prior incidents, constraints, and existing mitigations. Preserve independent failure generation where people are available; otherwise mark the list as a single perspective. Ask at most two decisive questions, such as “Which assumption would make the plan fail even if execution is competent?” or “What signal would appear before the harm becomes irreversible?”

## Procedure

1. **Freeze the plan and failure frame.** Write the plan and a future statement such as “It is [date], and this plan did not achieve its stated outcome.” **Visible output/state change:** publish the chosen action, success boundary, future horizon, and failure definition.
2. **Generate failure reasons independently.** List concrete reasons across operations, technology, people, market, finance, governance, and external conditions before debating them. **Visible output/state change:** publish a raw failure register while preserving distinct perspectives and marking each reason as imagined, observed, or assumed.
3. **Group causal paths.** Combine duplicates, separate causes from effects, and connect each material path to the plan assumption or dependency it breaks. Do not collapse different risks merely because they share an owner. **Visible output/state change:** publish grouped failure paths with their broken assumptions and evidence states.
4. **Select material paths.** Compare consequence, reversibility, time to detect, and controllability using ordinal language defined for this task. Keep more than one path when different failures would require different responses. **Visible output/state change:** publish a short attention list and explain why each path matters without fabricated likelihood or severity scores.
5. **Add signals and mitigations.** For each path, state the earliest observable leading signal, the prevention or mitigation, the owner, and the trigger for pausing, changing, or stopping the plan. **Visible output/state change:** publish an action-and-signal register linked to each failure path.
6. **Recheck the decision.** Ask whether the mitigations change the plan, its sequencing, scope, or go/no-go condition. Record the most important unknown and what evidence would alter the choice. **Visible output/state change:** publish a supported, provisional, or held recommendation with residual risks and a flip condition.

## Public output contract

Return the imagined failure, leading causes or paths, broken assumptions, early signals, mitigations, owners, and stop or change triggers. Distinguish imagined hypotheses from verified incident evidence. Do not claim that the exercise forecasts a probability. The Conclusion Card should state whether the plan remains acceptable, needs mitigation, or is held, and what observable condition would change that result; the trace can retain the full failure register.

## Evidence and uncertainty rules

Premortem reasons are deliberately generative. Treat them as hypotheses until supported by records, tests, comparable cases, or direct observation. Do not use vividness, seniority, or the number of people naming a risk as proof. Use ordinal attention labels only with a stated local meaning, and never multiply invented severity, occurrence, and detectability values. If a leading signal is not measurable or its timing is unknown, mark it unverified and do not present it as an early warning.

## Stop conditions

Stop when no concrete plan or failure boundary exists, when the imagined exercise would delay urgent containment, or when a material path has no safe mitigation and the plan would expose people to preventable harm. Hold the recommendation when the decisive assumption or signal cannot be checked before commitment. Escalate binding legal, clinical, safety, or security questions to the qualified owner.

## Complement handoff

Hand off to failure-mode-effects-analysis when failure paths should be tied to process steps, controls, and an ordinal priority register. Pass the public plan frame, grouped paths, signals, mitigations, and owners; do not pass invented scores or present imagined reasons as observed rates.
