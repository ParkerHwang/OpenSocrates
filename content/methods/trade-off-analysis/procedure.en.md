## Purpose

Use this procedure to make a choice transparent when viable options perform differently across criteria that cannot all be improved at once. It separates hard constraints from preferences, records why a criterion or weight matters, and shows what the selected option gives up. The result is a recommendation or a held comparison, not a claim that one score can settle a conflict between safety, equity, quality, cost, speed, or other values.

## Use when

Use it when two or more options compete across explicit objectives, stakeholder priorities, or measurable and non-measurable criteria. It fits procurement, design, policy, staffing, sequencing, and product decisions where the choice depends on trade-offs rather than one authoritative fact. It is especially useful when participants disagree about weights and need to see whether the disagreement changes the recommendation or only changes a secondary preference.

## Do not use when

Do not use it when only one feasible option exists, when a binding rule decides the action without discretion, or when the real question is whether more information is worth collecting. Do not turn an unmet safety, legal, rights, or minimum-performance requirement into a low score that another benefit can compensate for. Do not assign weights after seeing the outcome to make a preferred option win, and do not treat unsupported ratings as measurements.

## Inputs to establish

State the decision, options, stakeholders, criteria, hard constraints, minimum acceptable levels, time horizon, and implementation dependencies. For every rating or weight, record the source, owner, scale, date, and whether it is observed, computed, inferred, assumed, unverified, or conflicted. Separate ordinal judgments from defensible cardinal measures. Ask at most two decisive questions, such as “Which condition is a veto rather than a preference?” and “Whose stated priority supplies this weight?”

## Procedure

1. **Frame the choice.** Define the decision, feasible option set, time boundary, and what a satisfactory decision must deliver. **Visible output/state change:** publish a choice frame and mark an unknown option or boundary as unverified.
2. **Separate constraints and criteria.** List veto conditions, minimum thresholds, preferences, and outcomes that can be traded. Remove an option that fails a genuine hard constraint or explain why the constraint is not binding. **Visible output/state change:** publish a constraint register and a criteria register with separate status fields.
3. **Establish provenance.** Define the meaning and direction of each criterion, the rating scale, the source of each performance claim, and the origin of each weight. Use ordinal labels when a numeric scale is not defensible. **Visible output/state change:** publish a criteria-and-weight provenance map and mark contested inputs conflicted or assumed.
4. **Compare options.** Show each option against every material criterion, including missing evidence, distributional effects, and dependencies. Identify dominated options only when the comparison uses the same criteria and constraints. **Visible output/state change:** publish an option comparison with support states and a list of dominated or incomparable trade-offs.
5. **Test the recommendation.** Apply the agreed weights or a transparent qualitative comparison, then vary contested weights and material ratings within defensible bounds. Keep non-compensable criteria outside the additive score and record whose preference each scenario represents. **Visible output/state change:** publish a recommendation table with the selected option, rejected alternatives, concessions, and switching conditions.
6. **Set a held or supported conclusion.** State what the recommendation depends on, what risk is accepted, and what observation would flip it. If options, criteria, or decision-changing preferences remain missing, hold the comparison and name the smallest clarification needed. **Visible output/state change:** publish criteria/weight provenance, option comparison, dominated trade-offs, recommendation, uncertainties, and a Conclusion Card flip condition.

## Public output contract

Return the criteria, hard constraints, option comparison, provenance of ratings and weights, dominated or incomparable trade-offs, selected option, concessions, accepted risks, and flip condition. A computed score shows its scale, weights, operands, and units; an ordinal judgment is labelled as such. The card uses a concise recommendation, grounds with evidence states, remaining uncertainty, observable flip conditions, and an alternatives summary. Put detailed matrices and stakeholder disagreements in the trace.

## Evidence and uncertainty rules

Verified means an identifiable source directly supports the performance claim; computed means a reproducible transformation of stated ratings; inferred means a conclusion from labelled comparisons; assumed means an explicit preference or planning premise; unverified means an unchecked claim; conflicted means credible inputs disagree. Do not turn a stakeholder preference into an objective fact. Do not average unlike scales without a defensible mapping, hide a failed threshold in a total, or use a weight to disguise a missing fact. If a contested weight changes the option, show both outcomes and lower the conclusion.

## Stop conditions

Hold when the feasible set, a hard constraint, a decisive criterion, or the source of a decision-changing rating is missing. Hold when stakeholders have not supplied a material preference and no legitimate default exists. Stop when a binding condition is being traded against a benefit or when a single feasible option makes comparison unnecessary.

## Complement handoff

Hand off to sensitivity-analysis when the option comparison is complete but the recommendation may flip across defensible weights or performance ranges. Pass the public criteria, option matrix, provenance, constraints, and switching variables only; do not pass hidden preference negotiation or unsupported scores.
