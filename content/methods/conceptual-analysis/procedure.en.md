## Purpose

Use this procedure to turn an ambiguous or contested term into a usable decision definition without pretending that one dictionary entry settles the issue. It separates competing meanings, tests candidate conditions against examples and boundary cases, and records what follows from the chosen operational meaning. The result is a definition that supports an action, measurement, or policy while keeping residual ambiguity visible.

## Use when

Use it when a term such as safe, fair, quality, ready, trustworthy, sustainable, or successful carries the decision and different people may be using it differently. It fits policy, product requirements, evaluation criteria, contracts, research questions, and disagreements where the dispute is partly about meaning. It is also useful before argument mapping when a shared word hides separate claims.

## Do not use when

Do not use it when the relevant term already has a stable operational definition in an authoritative specification and the task is simply to apply or verify it. Do not use it to settle a factual dispute by rewriting a definition, and do not treat a preferred moral judgment as a discovered essence. A definition cannot replace evidence for whether a case meets it.

## Inputs to establish

Establish the target term, the decision or measurement it controls, the speaker or document using it, and the relevant context. Collect a small set of clear positive cases, clear negative cases, and up to three boundary cases. Record any authoritative definition, user preference, and practical consequence. Ask at most two questions, and only if the intended context or decision use cannot otherwise be bounded.

## Procedure

1. Publish the target concept, context, and decision it controls in one sentence. Mark whether the context came from the user, a governing document, or an assumption; this creates the visible analysis state.
2. Publish two to four competing meanings, each with its source or speaker and the outcome it would permit. Do not collapse different uses merely because they share a word.
3. Publish clear positive and negative cases, then map which candidate meanings classify each case. This exposes whether the disagreement is semantic, factual, or both.
4. Publish candidate necessary conditions and sufficient conditions separately. For each condition, state whether it is supported, proposed, or unverified; never treat a plausible condition as established.
5. Publish boundary tests and counterexamples. Show the case that most threatens the chosen meaning and whether the rule should be narrowed, broadened, or left unresolved.
6. Publish one operational meaning, its scope, exclusions, and residual ambiguity. State the consequence for the current decision, measurement, or requirement and make the resulting state change explicit.
7. Publish the concept-to-decision link and an observable flip condition. If no definition classifies the decisive case reliably, hold the judgment rather than selecting a label for convenience.

## Public output contract

Return `concept`, `competing_meanings`, `chosen_operational_meaning`, `necessary_conditions`, `sufficient_conditions`, `boundary_cases`, `residual_ambiguity`, `consequence`, `decision_consequence`, and `flip_condition`. The conclusion must say what the chosen meaning permits or rules out, not claim that the concept has a timeless essence. Keep at most three material assumptions and two unresolved uncertainties. Card grounds carry **verified**, **inferred**, **assumed**, **unverified**, or **conflicted** labels; examples are evidence only when their provenance and relevance are stated.

## Evidence and uncertainty rules

An authoritative definition supports how a term is used, not whether the world satisfies it. Distinguish a source-backed definition, a user-selected convention, and a proposed operationalization. A boundary case that cannot be classified is not proof that either side is right; it is evidence of residual ambiguity. If competing meanings lead to different high-stakes actions, disclose the conflict and lower or hold the conclusion until the authorized context is selected.

## Stop conditions

Stop and ask one blocking question when the target context is genuinely incompatible across plausible readings and the user’s decision cannot be safely bounded. Hold the result when the decisive case remains unclassifiable or when necessary evidence for applying the operational meaning is absent. Stop when the term is stable, the dispute is factual rather than semantic, and another method can handle the remaining evidence.

## Complement handoff

Hand off to `argument-mapping` when the chosen meaning exposes competing claims, shared premises, or objections that need structural separation. Pass only the selected operational meaning, the material boundary case, and the unresolved consequence. The complement should not repeat the full definition inventory.
