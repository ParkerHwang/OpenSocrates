## Purpose

Use this procedure when improving one technical or operational property appears to worsen another and a compromise is blocking progress. It states the desired ideal outcome, separates a technical contradiction from a physical contradiction, searches for separation or inventive moves, and uses resources already present in the system. The result is a candidate concept that reduces the contradiction and a test for whether the trade-off was actually removed, not a claim that an invention is guaranteed.

## Use when

Use it for product, process, service, or system design where “making A better makes B worse,” or where the same element seems to need incompatible properties at different times, places, conditions, or scales. It is useful when repeated tuning produces diminishing returns and the team can describe the improving and worsening parameters. Use the method for solution exploration after the problem and constraints are clear; combine it with systems or evidence work when the concept affects a wider feedback structure.

## Do not use when

Do not use it for primarily moral, evidentiary, or consent questions, or when stakeholder agreement is the real bottleneck. Do not invoke a principle by name without applying it to the actual contradiction. Do not recommend a candidate that violates safety, legal, accessibility, privacy, or physical constraints merely because it looks ideal. If the contradiction is only a preference trade-off with no technical mechanism, use a decision or values method instead.

## Inputs to establish

Describe the system, useful function, harmful effect, improving property, worsening property, available resources, operating conditions, and hard constraints. State the ideal result in plain language: the useful function remains while cost, harm, delay, or complexity is reduced. Ask at most two decisive questions, such as “Which property must improve and which worsens as a direct result?” or “Can the two demands be separated by time, place, condition, or scale?”

## Procedure

1. **State the ideal result.** Describe the useful function that should occur with fewer resources, harms, delays, or added parts; keep it as a direction, not a literal promise of zero cost. **Visible output/state change:** publish the ideal-result statement, system boundary, and constraints.
2. **Form the contradiction.** Write the technical form “improve X while preserving Y” and, when relevant, the physical form “the same element must be X and not-X under different conditions.” **Visible output/state change:** publish the contradiction and the evidence or assumption supporting each side.
3. **Try separation.** Check separation in time, space, condition, or part-versus-whole; also ask whether an interface, phase, or existing resource can carry one demand. **Visible output/state change:** publish the separation moves considered and why each is feasible, blocked, or unverified.
4. **Apply inventive moves.** Generate several concrete concepts using moves such as segmentation, extraction, asymmetry, merging, prior action, reversal, self-service, or a mediator. Explain how each move changes the contradiction rather than merely naming it. **Visible output/state change:** publish at least three candidate concepts with the contradiction they address.
5. **Search internal resources.** Identify unused time, space, information, energy, material, redundancy, or user action already available in the system. **Visible output/state change:** publish resource-to-concept links and any new burden or side effect.
6. **Screen and test.** Reject compromise concepts that leave the contradiction intact, then select a reversible test for the most promising candidate. **Visible output/state change:** publish the candidate, remaining trade-off, owner, safety screen, evidence needed, and flip condition; hold the result if the contradiction cannot be tested.

## Public output contract

Return the useful function, ideal result, technical or physical contradiction, separation moves, inventive concepts, internal resources, feasibility screen, and test. State whether a candidate removes, shifts, or merely compromises the contradiction. The Conclusion Card should name the concept worth testing and the observable condition that would change it; the trace can retain rejected moves. Do not claim novelty, patentability, or guaranteed improvement without external support.

## Evidence and uncertainty rules

Inventive principles generate hypotheses. They do not prove that a design works, is safe, or is novel. Keep the contradiction tied to observed requirements and distinguish measured behavior from assumptions. Do not fabricate ideality ratios, performance gains, patent precedent, or probability. If a candidate moves harm to another actor or lifecycle stage, show the side effect and lower or hold the conclusion. Safety and regulatory evidence takes precedence over an elegant concept.

## Stop conditions

Stop when the improving and worsening properties cannot be stated, the system boundary is unclear, or a candidate would violate a hard safety or legal constraint. Hold when every concept is a compromise or the decisive performance test is unavailable. Escalate specialist engineering, safety, accessibility, or privacy review before live deployment.

## Complement handoff

Hand off to systems-thinking when a candidate changes feedback, delays, actors, or resource flows beyond the local contradiction. Pass the public contradiction, candidate concepts, resources, side effects, and open test; the complement should map wider dynamics without treating the candidate as validated.
