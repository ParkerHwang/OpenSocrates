## Purpose

Use this procedure to examine a bundle of claims before accepting, repeating, or acting on it. It separates fact statements, interpretations, and opinions; checks the source and its incentives; tests framing and omissions; and inspects the inference from premises to conclusion. The result is a fair verdict—supported, conditionally supported, held, or rejected—with a clear distinction between “not verified” and “false.”

## Use when

Use it for reports, statistics, expert statements, persuasive memos, news claims, marketing material, or a recommendation whose support is disputed. It is appropriate when authority, selective examples, emotionally loaded language, hidden assumptions, or a leap from data to conclusion may matter. Use it for a claim bundle that needs a broad check and hand off to a more specific evidence or argument method when one narrow question clearly dominates.

## Do not use when

Do not use it as a substitute for a domain expert, a source verification service, a formal calculation, or a safety assessment. Do not reject a claim solely because its speaker has an interest, and do not accept it solely because the speaker is famous. A simple factual lookup or mechanical formatting task needs no critical procedure. If the claim is harmful advocacy that would be amplified by reconstructing it, summarize the relevant risk without strengthening the harmful case.

## Inputs to establish

Quote or paraphrase only the public claim under review, then identify its intended decision or action. Separate the main conclusion from its supporting reasons, examples, definitions, and requested standard of proof. Record source identity, date, directness, relevant expertise, incentives, missing comparison groups, and any material evidence that is available. Ask at most two decisive questions, such as “Which exact sentence would you act on?” or “What source directly supports the key factual premise?”

## Procedure

1. **Separate the claim bundle.** Mark each statement as fact, interpretation, prediction, value judgment, or recommendation, and choose the strongest claim that controls the decision. **Visible output/state change:** publish the claim map and the decision target; statements outside scope are not silently evaluated.
2. **Check the source.** Examine whether the source is identifiable, relevantly expert, current enough, traceable to primary material, and transparent about incentives. **Visible output/state change:** publish source findings with verified, unverified, or conflicted labels; source weakness lowers support but does not alone prove falsity.
3. **Check framing and omissions.** Ask why this example, period, denominator, comparison, and emotional vocabulary were selected. Look for excluded cases, survivorship, base-rate changes, and an alternative frame that would alter the decision. **Visible output/state change:** publish material omissions and a fair reframe.
4. **Check the inference.** Trace the premises to the conclusion and test relevance, sufficiency, scope, and common errors such as hasty generalization, false dilemma, straw man, or ad hominem substitution. **Visible output/state change:** publish supported links and identified gaps rather than a generic “logical” score.
5. **Build the counterargument.** State the strongest reasonable objection and the evidence it would need; distinguish a genuine defeater from a merely different preference. **Visible output/state change:** publish the counterargument, response, and any conclusion change or unresolved conflict.
6. **Set the verdict and next check.** Integrate source, framing, inference, and counterargument results. **Visible output/state change:** publish supported, conditionally supported, held, or rejected; list what remains unverified and the smallest check that could change the verdict.

## Public output contract

Return the strongest claim, source assessment, material framing or omission, inference gap, strongest counterargument, and verdict. Use evidence-state labels and never present a reputation judgment as factual support. “Unverified” means the required check was not completed or unavailable; “rejected” requires a material contradiction or decisive defect. Include a Conclusion Card flip condition and keep detailed claim-ground links in the trace.

## Evidence and uncertainty rules

Source credibility, framing, and logical structure are separate axes. A weak source can still make a true statement, and a prestigious source can make a flawed inference. Do not fabricate source dates, expertise, sample quality, or a numerical confidence score. Mark a missing denominator, omitted comparison, or untested premise as unverified. If credible material conflicts, use conflicted and lower or hold the conclusion. Distinguish “I cannot verify this” from “this is false.”

## Stop conditions

Stop and hold when the central claim cannot be stated, the source or key premise is unavailable, or a decisive gap cannot be bounded. Stop before repeating a high-stakes claim as fact when its material support is unverified. For harmful content, avoid reconstructing persuasive details and state only the safe evaluation needed.

## Complement handoff

Hand off to argument-mapping when claim-ground-objection links need a more explicit structure. Pass the public claim map, identified gap, and counterargument; the complement should not restart source or framing checks already recorded.
