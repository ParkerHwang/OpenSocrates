## Purpose

Use this procedure to compare a decision against a real baseline when benefits and costs can be described in comparable terms, while keeping values that should not be traded away visible. It produces a bounded economic comparison, not a claim that every human, ecological, safety, or distributional effect has a price. The conclusion may be numerical, qualitative, or held when a decisive input is missing.

## Use when

Use it for a policy, programme, product investment, operational change, or project with at least two viable options or a meaningful do-nothing baseline. It fits decisions where timing, recurring effects, affected groups, and resource commitments matter. It is useful when a decision maker needs to see net implication alongside who pays, who benefits, which values remain outside the calculation, and which assumptions could change the choice.

## Do not use when

Do not use it when a binding rule already determines the action, when only one feasible option remains, or when the request is a mechanical calculation with fixed inputs and no judgment. Do not force a monetary value onto a dignity, rights, safety, ecological threshold, or other non-compensable value. If the baseline, alternatives, or decisive cost and benefit evidence cannot be bounded, use a held outcome or hand off to a method that can structure the unresolved question.

## Inputs to establish

State the decision, the decision owner, the baseline, the viable options, the time horizon, and the affected groups. List benefits, costs, transfers, risks, and values that must remain separate from a net total. Record the source, date, unit, and evidence state for each material input. Use a defensible discount rule only when it is supplied or supported by the decision context; otherwise show time-separated values. Ask at most two decisive questions, such as “What happens if no option is chosen?” and “Which value cannot be compensated by money?”

## Procedure

1. **Frame the comparison.** Define the choice, baseline, options, time horizon, and decision boundary; separate the option from the recommendation. **Visible output/state change:** publish a decision frame with the baseline and any missing boundary labelled unverified.
2. **Inventory consequences.** For each option, list direct and indirect benefits, costs, transfers between groups, implementation timing, and possible harms. Keep benefits that are easy to measure from benefits that are merely asserted. **Visible output/state change:** create an option consequence register with one row or item for every material effect.
3. **Classify comparability.** Mark each effect as monetizable, measurable but not safely monetizable, non-compensable, or unresolved. Attach a unit, source, calculation basis, and evidence label to any value that enters a total. **Visible output/state change:** publish a labelled valuation map and remove unsupported values from the net calculation.
4. **Set the calculation boundary.** Record the horizon, timing convention, discount rule, price basis, and treatment of uncertainty. Do not supply a default discount rate, probability, or price when the user has not provided a defensible basis. **Visible output/state change:** publish a calculation specification or a held note explaining which decisive parameter is missing.
5. **Compare and stress the result.** Compute a net implication or benefit-cost comparison only from stated inputs, show the expression and units, then vary the material assumptions or use qualitative low/base/high cases with provenance. Report distributional effects separately from aggregate totals. **Visible output/state change:** publish a comparison with sensitivity findings, non-compensable constraints, and the first observable switching condition.
6. **Set the public conclusion.** Choose supported, provisional, or held wording. If a missing input could change the option, state the smallest safe check and do not present the arithmetic as a decision. **Visible output/state change:** publish options, benefits, costs, distribution, uncertainties, net implication, and a Conclusion Card flip condition.

## Public output contract

Return the baseline and options, benefits and costs with units and evidence states, distribution across affected groups, non-compensable values, uncertainty, and the net implication. A computed result includes its expression, inputs, units, and rounding rule where relevant. A qualitative result explains why comparison is limited. The card contains one conclusion sentence, one to five grounds, zero to two material uncertainties, one to two observable flip conditions, and a compact alternatives summary. Keep detailed calculations and source dates in the trace.

## Evidence and uncertainty rules

Label direct source support as verified only when the source actually supports the stated effect. Label arithmetic as computed, consequences inferred from labelled inputs as inferred, and planning assumptions as assumed. Use unverified for a checkable value not checked and conflicted when credible inputs disagree. Do not hide a transfer as a social benefit, count the same effect twice, or infer certainty from a precise-looking total. If a non-monetary threshold is breached, it remains a constraint even when the net total is positive. Never invent a price, probability, discount rate, or distributional claim.

## Stop conditions

Hold when the baseline, viable options, or a decision-changing cost, benefit, threshold, or time rule is missing. Hold when credible sources conflict on a decisive input and no precedence rule is justified. If the choice involves an immediate safety or rights constraint, surface that constraint and seek qualified review before comparing aggregate totals.

## Complement handoff

Hand off to sensitivity-analysis when the comparison is defined but the recommendation may flip across defensible input or weight ranges. Pass the public baseline, option register, calculation specification, non-compensable constraints, and switching variables only; do not pass unsupported prices or hidden deliberation.
