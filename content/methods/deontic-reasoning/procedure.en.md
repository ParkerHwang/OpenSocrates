## Purpose

Use this procedure to distinguish what an actor must do, may do, must not do, or may do only under an exception. It makes candidate duties, permissions, prohibitions, rights, and policy conditions explicit, checks their source and scope, and exposes conflicts before practical preferences are applied. The result is a bounded set of permitted actions and a clear status for every obligation; it is not a substitute for authoritative legal or professional advice.

## Use when

Use it when a decision involves a policy, contract, consent, rights, authority, safety duty, professional standard, or conflict between “good outcome” and “allowed action.” It is useful when recommendations blur requirements with preferences, when an exception may matter, or when different sources appear to impose different duties. Use the governing text or named authority when available and state the jurisdiction, organization, role, and time scope that make the rule relevant.

## Do not use when

Do not invent a legal duty, right, permission, or prohibition from general moral intuition or a vague policy summary. Do not use it to settle a purely empirical cause, a technical design contradiction, or a preference with no normative constraint. Do not let a desirable outcome justify a prohibited act. If the source is unavailable, ambiguous, outdated, or outside the stated authority, mark the rule unverified and seek the responsible owner rather than presenting it as binding.

## Inputs to establish

State the decision, actors, candidate actions, role and authority of each actor, relevant source or policy, jurisdiction or organizational scope, effective period, exceptions, and harms of misclassification. Separate explicit text from interpretation and from a proposed ethical principle. Ask at most two decisive questions, such as “Which source actually governs this actor and action?” or “Is there an exception or consent condition that changes the status?”

## Procedure

1. **Fix the action set and authority.** List the realistic actions and identify who could perform or authorize each; exclude options outside the decision boundary. **Visible output/state change:** publish the action list, actor roles, and authority scope.
2. **Extract candidate norms.** For each source, state whether it appears to require, permit, prohibit, or exempt an action, and quote or safely identify the relevant public basis. **Visible output/state change:** publish an obligation register with source, scope, effective period, and evidence state.
3. **Check hierarchy and conditions.** Compare applicable sources, exceptions, consent, role limits, and precedence rules; distinguish a recommendation from a duty. **Visible output/state change:** publish the active rule, conditions, and conflicts, with ambiguous interpretations marked unverified.
4. **Resolve conflicts cautiously.** When duties collide, identify the governing precedence if the source establishes one; otherwise state the conflict and choose the least harmful permitted interim action only with qualified authority. **Visible output/state change:** publish a conflict disposition or a held status rather than silently choosing a favorite rule.
5. **Filter permitted options.** Remove prohibited actions, preserve required actions, and leave genuinely permitted choices open for practical comparison. **Visible output/state change:** publish permitted, required, prohibited, and unresolved options with the reason and source status for each.
6. **Set the practical next step.** Within the permitted set, state the next action, owner, evidence needed, and condition that would change the normative status. **Visible output/state change:** publish the conclusion, hold or escalation path, and flip condition.

## Public output contract

Return candidate obligations, permissions, prohibitions, exemptions, governing sources, scope, conflicts, permitted options, and the unresolved question. Distinguish verified source text, inferred interpretation, assumed context, and unverified authority. A Conclusion Card may state “permitted under the stated source” or “cannot establish permission yet,” but must not claim a universal legal conclusion without authority. Keep full source reasoning and alternatives in the trace.

## Evidence and uncertainty rules

Normative status depends on source, scope, role, timing, and conditions, not only on consequences. A policy suggestion is not automatically a duty; a common practice is not automatically a permission. Do not fabricate citations, jurisdiction, effective dates, consent, or hierarchy. If sources conflict, mark the status conflicted and hold or escalate unless an authoritative precedence rule resolves it. Safety, rights, and privacy constraints are not traded away by a favorable outcome.

## Stop conditions

Stop when the governing source or authority is unknown, the action or actor is undefined, an exception could change the result but is unverified, or a high-stakes status would require professional interpretation. Hold the action rather than call it permitted or required. Escalate to the responsible legal, policy, clinical, safety, or governance owner when applicable.

## Complement handoff

Hand off to reflective-equilibrium when a legitimate hard case exposes tension between principles and case judgments after binding rules and authority have been checked. Pass the public norms, scope, conflict, and permitted set; the complement must not invent a new duty.
