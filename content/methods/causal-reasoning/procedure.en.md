## Purpose

Use this procedure to decide whether a factor plausibly changes an outcome, rather than merely appearing alongside it. It separates association from causation, makes the mechanism inspectable, and identifies what would have happened under a relevant counterfactual. The result is a bounded account whose wording matches the evidence: established, contributory, associated, or held.

## Use when

Use it when the user asks whether A caused B, whether an intervention produced an effect, or how a policy, feature, behavior, or event changed a result. It is especially useful when data show a tempting before-and-after pattern, several explanations fit the outcome, or a recommendation depends on predicting what would change if one factor were altered. Use qualitative observations, experiments, administrative records, or a carefully described case; a large dataset is not required.

## Do not use when

Do not use this as the primary procedure for a purely descriptive summary, a simple calculation, or a choice governed by a binding rule. Do not turn a recurring, self-reinforcing pattern into a single-event explanation; hand it to systems-thinking or causal-loop-diagramming. Do not assert causation when the outcome, exposure, timing, or comparison is undefined. If a safety, medical, legal, or financial decision depends on an unverified causal claim, state the limitation and seek qualified review rather than upgrading the language.

## Inputs to establish

State the causal claim in one sentence: what factor, for whom, over what time window, is said to change which outcome. Record the direction and unit of the outcome where possible, the observed comparison or counterfactual, the timing of exposure and outcome, and the source or observation supporting each material fact. Name plausible confounders, reverse-causality paths, selection effects, and any mechanism that connects exposure to outcome. Ask at most two decisive questions, such as “What comparison would have existed without A?” or “When did A occur relative to B?”

## Procedure

1. **Fix the claim and comparison.** Rewrite the question as “For [population] during [period], would changing A change B compared with [specified alternative]?” **Visible output/state change:** publish the one-sentence causal claim, population, time window, and comparison; if any is missing, mark the claim provisional.
2. **Check covariation and timing.** Compare the available observations without treating a sequence or correlation as proof. Confirm that A varies with B in the relevant cases and that A precedes the outcome; record mismatches and missing observations. **Visible output/state change:** add a small association/timing finding labelled verified, computed, inferred, or unverified.
3. **Map alternatives.** List credible confounders, reverse-causality paths, selection effects, measurement changes, and coincidence. For each, state what evidence would distinguish it from the proposed claim. **Visible output/state change:** publish an alternatives list and mark each path as checked, unresolved, or not applicable.
4. **Test the mechanism.** Describe the shortest plausible A-to-B pathway and identify the intermediate observations that should appear if it operates. Separate a mechanism supported by direct evidence from a mechanism supplied only by a plausible story. **Visible output/state change:** publish the mechanism chain with support states and downgrade the claim if a necessary link is missing.
5. **Examine the counterfactual.** Ask what B would likely have been without A, using a control, interrupted trend, natural comparison, repeated case, or explicit qualitative contrast when available. Do not invent a numerical effect or probability. **Visible output/state change:** record the counterfactual basis and its limits; if no defensible comparison exists, set the outcome to held or provisional.
6. **Set the conclusion and next check.** Weigh timing, covariation, alternative explanations, mechanism, and counterfactual together; a single passed check never overrides a decisive gap. **Visible output/state change:** publish the causal conclusion, strength wording, remaining uncertainty, and one observable flip condition or next test.

## Public output contract

Return a concise causal claim, observed comparison, and mechanism description. Show main confounders or alternatives, the counterfactual basis, and conclusion strength using labels such as verified, computed, inferred, assumed, unverified, or conflicted. Include what would change the conclusion. A strong causal statement requires support for material links and no unresolved decisive alternative; otherwise use “may contribute,” “is consistent with,” or “an association is observed.” Map grounds to the Conclusion Card and keep detail for the trace.

## Evidence and uncertainty rules

Correlation, temporal precedence, or a plausible mechanism alone is not decisive. A source directly supporting an outcome is not automatically evidence that the exposure caused it. Mark user reports, inferred mechanisms, and unmeasured comparisons as assumed or unverified. If credible sources disagree, label the relevant ground conflicted and lower or hold the conclusion. Never fabricate effect sizes, confidence intervals, probabilities, sample quality, or a passed counterfactual. A conclusion may remain useful as a provisional hypothesis only when the missing check and its practical consequence are explicit.

## Stop conditions

Stop and hold when the causal target or comparison cannot be defined, when timing is unknown in a way that permits reverse causation, or when a credible alternative explanation remains equally capable of producing the outcome. Stop before recommending a high-stakes intervention if the decisive causal evidence is unverified. State the missing input and the smallest safe check instead of filling the gap with confidence language.

## Complement handoff

Hand off to falsificationism when the causal account has a clear risky prediction and a decisive disproof test can be stated. Pass only the public claim, mechanism, key alternative, and proposed falsifier; do not repeat the full procedure or expose hidden reasoning.
