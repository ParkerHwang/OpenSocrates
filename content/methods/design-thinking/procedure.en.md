## Purpose

Use this procedure to turn observed human needs into solution concepts that can be tested before a large commitment. It separates what people say from what their behavior and context reveal, frames one actionable need, generates several options before selecting, and makes a small prototype answer a specific question. The output is a user-centered problem definition, a testable concept, and a learning rule—not an assumption that a requested feature is the need.

## Use when

Use it for products, services, workflows, or experiences where the user’s situation, emotion, access, trust, or social context shapes success. It is useful when a request is expressed as a feature, when stakeholders disagree about the real problem, or when the solution space is open and a small experiment can reveal whether a need is genuine. Use observations, interviews, support records, journey fragments, and direct user feedback, while labelling interpretation separately from observation.

## Do not use when

Do not use it to bypass a binding safety, legal, accessibility, or security requirement, or to replace a proven technical diagnosis. Do not infer a universal need from one anecdote, demographic label, or the loudest request. Do not prototype a harmful or high-risk change with real users before appropriate safeguards and review. If the user, situation, or desired progress cannot be observed or bounded, hold the concept rather than filling the gap with empathy language.

## Inputs to establish

State the target user or affected group, situation, observed behavior, friction, desired progress, constraints, and decision the work must inform. Separate direct observations and quotes from interpretations and solution requests. Record access, consent, privacy, safety, and feasibility limits. Ask at most two decisive questions, such as “What happens when this need is unmet?” or “Which observed behavior would make the proposed need wrong?”

## Procedure

1. **Gather the human situation.** Organize observations around context, action, emotion, workaround, and consequence; keep individual evidence distinct from generalization. **Visible output/state change:** publish an evidence-labelled user situation and the unmet progress it may represent.
2. **Frame a point of view.** Write “A person in [situation] needs to [progress] because [observed reason],” without embedding a preferred solution. **Visible output/state change:** publish one bounded need statement and mark unsupported interpretations as inferred or assumed.
3. **Generate distinct concepts.** Produce several ways to address the need, including a low-tech, service, policy, and product possibility where relevant; delay selection until the set is meaningfully different. **Visible output/state change:** publish at least three concept candidates and the need each one serves.
4. **Choose a minimum prototype.** Select the smallest reversible artifact or enactment that can test the most uncertain part of the point of view. State the question, target participant, exposure boundary, and what will count as disconfirming feedback. **Visible output/state change:** publish a prototype brief and safety/consent guardrails.
5. **Test and interpret.** Observe behavior and reported experience against the predeclared question; do not treat enthusiasm, politeness, or completion alone as proof of durable value. **Visible output/state change:** publish test observations, evidence states, and whether the point of view is supported, revised, or rejected.
6. **Decide the next iteration.** Choose to refine, expand, pause, or discard the concept, and state what new evidence is required before a larger commitment. **Visible output/state change:** publish the selected concept, learning, owner, next check, and flip condition.

## Public output contract

Return the observed user situation, desired progress, point-of-view need, concept set, selected prototype, test question, disconfirmation rule, and next decision. Show direct observation separately from inferred need and assumed preference. The Conclusion Card should state whether the concept is supported for the tested context and what user evidence would change that; the trace may contain participant-safe detail and rejected concepts.

## Evidence and uncertainty rules

A stated feature request is evidence of a request, not proof of the underlying job. One enthusiastic participant, a convenient persona, or a successful prototype interaction does not establish broad demand. Do not fabricate user counts, conversion rates, satisfaction scores, or representativeness. Protect consent and privacy, avoid exposing sensitive user stories, and mark unobserved populations unverified. If the prototype test is inaccessible, unsafe, or too small to answer the question, hold the recommendation.

## Stop conditions

Stop when the situation, affected person, or desired progress is undefined, when the concept embeds an untested solution assumption, or when the test could cause preventable harm. Hold a scale recommendation when the prototype did not test the deciding uncertainty. Escalate accessibility, safety, legal, or privacy risks before exposing users.

## Complement handoff

Hand off to jobs-to-be-done when the situation needs a sharper account of functional, emotional, and social progress and the forces that support or block switching. Pass the public situation, observed progress, concept question, and evidence states; the complement should not repeat ideation or invent a user segment.
