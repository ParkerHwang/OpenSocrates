## Purpose

Use this procedure when competing interpretations, concepts, or policies need to be judged by the practical differences they make. It identifies the object under dispute, chooses an appropriate practical standard, derives observable consequences for each option, checks whether the differences are real and material, and proposes a reversible test where possible. The result is a context-bound choice or a finding that the alternatives are practically equivalent—not a claim that whatever feels useful is true.

## Use when

Use it when an abstract argument is circling without changing action, when teams must choose between frameworks, or when a policy or interpretation should be evaluated by how it works in a specified setting. Choose the relevant strand deliberately: observable consequences for a concept, problem-solving performance for a tool, or lived meaning for a personal commitment. State whose outcomes matter, over what horizon, and what rights or safety constraints remain non-negotiable.

## Do not use when

Do not use short-run convenience to erase rights, safety, truth conditions, or binding duties. Do not infer that a belief is factual because it comforts someone, or that an interpretation is true because it produced one desired result. Do not use it for a mechanical calculation or when the practical consequences are not distinguishable. If the test would be irreversible, harmful, or unauthorized, compare the consequences conceptually and hold the choice until a safe check exists.

## Inputs to establish

State the competing interpretations or options, object type, decision, affected people, practical criterion, time horizon, constraints, current evidence, and available reversible tests. Separate consequences that are observed from those inferred or assumed. Ask at most two decisive questions, such as “What would we do differently if we accepted each interpretation?” or “Which outcome counts as success for the affected people?”

## Procedure

1. **Fix the dispute and standard.** Name what is being judged and choose whether the relevant test is observable effect, problem-solving performance, or lived practical meaning. **Visible output/state change:** publish the options, criterion, scope, and non-negotiable constraints.
2. **Derive practical consequences.** For each option, state changes in action, prediction, experience, resource use, relationships, or follow-on decisions. **Visible output/state change:** publish a consequence map and mark direct observations versus inference.
3. **Check for material difference.** Ask whether the options actually lead to different observable consequences in this context; if not, record practical equivalence rather than extending the debate. **Visible output/state change:** publish the difference or no-difference finding and the evidence gap.
4. **Evaluate performance and distribution.** Compare the options against the chosen criterion, the time horizon, affected groups, reversibility, and side effects. Do not let short-term utility silently override rights or safety. **Visible output/state change:** publish a bounded comparison with evidence states and unresolved trade-offs.
5. **Choose a reversible test.** When practical, specify a small test, observation window, threshold in plain language, and decision rule; if no safe test exists, state why. **Visible output/state change:** publish the test or the reason for a held conceptual judgment.
6. **Set the context-bound conclusion.** Choose the option, declare equivalence, or hold when decisive consequences are unverified. State what change in context would require re-evaluation. **Visible output/state change:** publish the current choice, limits, owner, and flip condition.

## Public output contract

Return the interpretations or options, practical criterion, observable consequences, material differences, distribution and side effects, reversible test, and choice or equivalence finding. Distinguish “works for this purpose and context” from “is true in every context.” The Conclusion Card should state the bounded practical conclusion and what observable result would change it; keep detailed consequence comparisons in the trace.

## Evidence and uncertainty rules

Practical consequences are evidence about usefulness or action, not automatic proof of truth. Reported satisfaction, one successful trial, or short-term output may be incomplete. Do not fabricate outcomes, costs, time horizons, or causal effects. Keep rights, safety, consent, and binding duties as constraints rather than variables to optimize away. If a consequence is predicted but untested, label it inferred or unverified; if options differ only rhetorically, say so without pretending to decide a factual dispute.

## Stop conditions

Stop when the options or standard cannot be stated, when no practical difference can be identified, or when the only proposed test is irreversible or unsafe. Hold the choice when the decisive consequence is unavailable and would materially alter the action. Escalate normative, safety, or legal constraints to the responsible authority.

## Complement handoff

Hand off to lean-startup when a market or product hypothesis can be reduced to a safe minimum test and a learning threshold. Pass the practical options, consequence map, test question, and constraints; the complement should not treat short-run usefulness as universal truth.
