## Purpose

Use this procedure to make a judgment’s boundary visible. It identifies who and what the current framing includes, who is affected but excluded, and how changing the boundary changes the conclusion. The aim is not to produce a longer stakeholder list; it is to expose the ownership, benefit, cost, authority, time horizon, jurisdiction, and system edge assumptions that make the current answer look inevitable. Keep descriptive claims (what is happening) separate from normative claims (what ought to count or be done).

## Use when

Use it when an answer may change depending on which actors count, whose outcomes are measured, what geography or time period is included, who owns the decision, or which externalities are treated as out of scope. It is especially useful for policy, product, organizational, public-interest, and system-change questions where a technically correct answer for one group can impose costs on another. A bounded comparative question can use this method when the comparison itself hides an omitted population or time horizon.

## Do not use when

Do not use it when the scope, decision owner, affected population, jurisdiction, and time horizon are already explicit and the remaining task is only to compare options or verify a source. Do not turn a purely mechanical request into a boundary exercise. Do not infer an affected group merely because it is imaginable; mark it as a hypothesis until the task or evidence makes the connection relevant.

## Inputs to establish

Establish the decision question, decision owner, intended beneficiaries, current inclusion rule, time horizon, geography or jurisdiction, resources counted, and outcome measure. Name up to three missing facts that could materially change the boundary. If the user has not supplied a boundary, state a reasonable provisional one and ask at most one concise question only when different answers would change the recommendation. Distinguish user-provided facts, checked sources, inferred effects, and value choices.

## Procedure

1. Publish a one-sentence decision question and the provisional boundary: who, what, where, when, and for which outcome. State whether the boundary is user-provided or assumed; this creates the visible framing state.
2. Publish an included-set record with actors, purposes, knowledge, resources, and decision rights. Attach a short reason for each inclusion so the boundary is auditable rather than merely asserted.
3. Publish an omitted-but-affected record. Separate direct effects, indirect effects, and people who could challenge the framing; mark uncertain entries as hypotheses rather than facts.
4. Publish a fact/value split: list descriptive observations separately from judgments about whose benefit, cost, voice, or right should count. This state change prevents a value choice from masquerading as a neutral system edge.
5. Publish a benefit-and-cost comparison across the included and omitted groups, using only material effects. Record an evidence label for each effect and mark unverified effects as unresolved.
6. Publish one or more boundary variants, such as a wider time horizon, a different jurisdiction, or an added affected group. For each variant, state whether the conclusion, option order, risk, or completion criterion changes.
7. Publish the current boundary, the consequence of the most decision-relevant change, and an observable flip condition. If a decisive boundary fact is missing, change the conclusion state to provisional or held instead of filling it with speculation.

## Public output contract

Return a compact, card-ready result with `current_boundary`, `omitted_boundary`, `benefit_and_cost_effects`, `boundary_variant`, `consequence_of_changing_it`, and `flip_condition`. The top line states the current boundary and whether it supports, weakens, or holds the judgment. Include no more than three material assumptions and no more than two unresolved uncertainties. In the card, grounds use the labels **verified**, **computed**, **inferred**, **assumed**, **unverified**, or **conflicted**; alternatives summarize the relevant boundary variants rather than listing every stakeholder.

## Evidence and uncertainty rules

An affected group is not evidence of an effect by itself. Use a source, user-supplied document, reproducible calculation, or clearly labeled inference for each material benefit or cost. Do not treat a majority view or decision owner’s preference as proof that an omitted group is immaterial. If boundary variants produce incompatible conclusions, label the judgment **conflicted** or **held** until the governing scope is chosen by the authorized decision owner. A missing decisive boundary fact supports only a provisional conclusion.

## Stop conditions

Stop and ask one blocking question when two plausible boundaries lead to materially different actions and neither can be selected safely from the user’s purpose or an authoritative rule. Hold the result when an affected group, jurisdiction, or time horizon is decisive but cannot be identified. Stop the method when the boundary is explicit and no material omitted actor or edge remains; hand off to the method that addresses the actual comparison or evidence task.

## Complement handoff

Hand off to `stakeholder-analysis` only as a focused complement when the boundary review reveals material power, consent, implementation, or conflict questions. Pass the published included/omitted sets, the selected boundary variant, and the unresolved effect that requires stakeholder analysis. Do not activate a second full procedure or repeat the boundary inventory.
