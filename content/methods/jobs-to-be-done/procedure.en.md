## Purpose

Use this procedure to understand the progress a person is trying to make in a specific situation, rather than treating a feature, demographic segment, or product category as the need. It identifies the struggling moment, functional, emotional, and social jobs, forces that push and pull behavior, habits and anxieties that resist change, and a success measure that can be observed. The result is a bounded job statement and a product or service implication, not a universal portrait of a customer.

## Use when

Use it when product choice, switching, retention, adoption, or non-consumption is poorly explained by feature lists or demographics. It fits questions such as why a person hires one service, keeps an imperfect workaround, delays a change, or chooses not to buy. Use interviews, observed journeys, support conversations, usage traces, and competitor or non-consumption examples. Keep the situation and desired progress specific enough that a different solution could satisfy the same job.

## Do not use when

Do not use it as a moral-purpose analysis, a root-cause diagnosis for a technical failure, or a substitute for direct user research. Do not infer a job from age, industry, or a feature request alone. Do not claim that one interview represents a whole market. If the person’s situation, progress, or decision is unknown, hold the job statement and ask for the smallest decisive observation rather than inventing a persona.

## Inputs to establish

State the person or actor, triggering situation, desired progress, current workaround or alternative, functional outcome, emotional experience, social meaning, forces for and against switching, constraints, and success signal. Separate direct language and observed behavior from interpretation. Ask at most two decisive questions, such as “What are they trying to accomplish at that moment?” or “What makes the current alternative hard to leave?”

## Procedure

1. **Fix the struggling moment.** Describe when the person begins looking for progress, what happened before, and what consequence matters. **Visible output/state change:** publish a situation statement with actor, trigger, context, and evidence state.
2. **Write the desired progress.** Complete “When [situation], I want to [make progress] so I can [desired outcome],” without naming a product. **Visible output/state change:** publish a job statement and mark inferred motives separately from observed intent.
3. **Separate job dimensions.** Describe functional work, emotional experience, social or identity signal, and any supporting constraints. **Visible output/state change:** publish the three-dimensional job profile with verified, inferred, assumed, or unverified labels.
4. **Map switching forces.** Record the push from the current problem, pull of the alternative, habit of the current behavior, and anxiety about change; include non-consumption as a real alternative. **Visible output/state change:** publish a force map and the evidence needed to distinguish a stated preference from behavior.
5. **Define success and alternatives.** State what progress would be observable, over what period, for whom, and how existing products, workarounds, or no purchase meet or fail the job. **Visible output/state change:** publish a success measure and alternative comparison without fabricated market shares or conversion rates.
6. **Choose the implication and check.** Select the product promise, onboarding change, feature focus, or research test that addresses the job, and state the observation that could disconfirm it. **Visible output/state change:** publish the provisional implication, owner, next test, and flip condition.

## Public output contract

Return the struggling situation, desired progress, functional/emotional/social job, switching forces, alternatives including non-consumption, success measure, and product or service implication. Keep direct evidence separate from interpretation. The Conclusion Card should state the job addressed in this context and what user behavior would change the recommendation; detailed force evidence and segment limits belong in the trace.

## Evidence and uncertainty rules

A feature is not a job, and a demographic segment is not a situation. Treat interview language as reported intent and observed switching or workaround behavior as a different evidence type. Do not invent sample size, market demand, emotional state, or conversion impact. Jobs can vary by context; do not generalize beyond the observed situation. If the desired progress is inferred and no behavior or direct account supports it, mark the conclusion provisional and state the next observation.

## Stop conditions

Stop when the triggering situation, actor, progress, or success signal cannot be stated, when the job is only a feature restatement, or when a high-stakes service change would rely on unverified user needs. Hold the product implication until the decisive behavior or alternative can be checked. Protect privacy and consent in user evidence.

## Complement handoff

Hand off to design-thinking when the job needs observation-led concept generation, prototyping, and user testing. Pass the public situation, job dimensions, forces, success signal, and evidence gaps; the complement should not treat the job statement as a verified market truth.
