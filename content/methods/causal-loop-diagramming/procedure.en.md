## Purpose

Use this procedure to turn a defensible dynamic explanation into a compact causal-loop diagram. It names variables, signs each directional link, distinguishes reinforcing from balancing loops, and marks delays that can make an intervention appear ineffective or create overshoot. The diagram is a public hypothesis about structure, not a proof or a forecast. Its value is to make a feedback story inspectable and to connect one possible intervention to an observable check.

## Use when

Use it when a problem repeats, accelerates, decays, oscillates, or resists a policy because effects return through the system. It fits questions about why demand feeds demand, why pressure creates short-term relief but long-term backlog, or why a corrective action is later undone. It is suitable for a small set of named variables and a clear time horizon. Use it as a focused complement when systems-thinking has already established a wider boundary and the user needs the loop structure made explicit.

## Do not use when

Do not use it for a one-time event, a simple linear mechanism, or a list of correlations with no defensible direction. Do not draw a loop only because the same word appears at both ends; every link needs a meaning and a time context. Do not use a diagram to settle a high-stakes causal claim without checking the links. If variables cannot be defined as quantities or conditions that can increase or decrease, stop and use a different structure.

## Inputs to establish

Set the focal behavior, time horizon, system boundary, and a short list of variables. For each variable, record what an increase or decrease means, the evidence or assumption supporting its direction, and any delay. Identify the intervention or decision whose consequences the loop should illuminate. Ask at most two decisive questions, such as “Which variable changes first in the observed pattern?” or “What delayed response could reverse the short-term effect?”

## Procedure

1. **Fix the pattern and boundary.** State the behavior over time and exclude unrelated factors; ensure the target is a dynamic pattern rather than a single event. **Visible output/state change:** publish the focal pattern, time horizon, and boundary, or mark the diagram held when they cannot be fixed.
2. **Define variables.** Convert vague events into named variables that can move up or down, and write the operational meaning beside each. **Visible output/state change:** publish a variable register with units or qualitative direction and mark unsupported variables as assumed.
3. **Sign the links.** For each proposed arrow, state whether an increase in the source tends to increase or decrease the target under the stated conditions. Record direct evidence, an accepted mechanism, or an explicit assumption. **Visible output/state change:** publish signed links with a support state and remove links whose direction is unknowable.
4. **Trace the loops.** Follow closed paths and classify each as reinforcing when change feeds more change in the same direction, or balancing when the path counteracts a deviation. Name the loop by its behavior, not by an unexplained letter. **Visible output/state change:** publish the loop descriptions and the link that makes each loop depend on its sign.
5. **Mark delays and intervention.** Add delays where information, action, or consequence arrives later, then identify how the delay can produce overshoot, oscillation, or policy resistance. Place the candidate intervention on the loop it is meant to alter. **Visible output/state change:** publish delay notes, intended effect, side effect, and the first observable signal to monitor.
6. **Check and hand off.** Compare the diagram with the observed pattern and state what evidence would falsify a link or change the loop classification. If a decisive link is absent, hold the structural conclusion. **Visible output/state change:** publish a supported or provisional diagram, a flip condition, and the next safe check.

## Public output contract

Return a compact diagram in readable text such as “training ↑ → confidence ↑ → practice ↑ → skill ↑ (reinforcing)” or “backlog ↑ → wait time ↑ → abandonment ↑ → backlog ↓ (balancing).” Include the variable definitions, loop type, delays, intervention implication, side effect, and evidence state. Keep assumptions visibly distinct from verified observations. The Conclusion Card should show the main loop-level implication and an observable flip condition; the full map belongs in the trace.

## Evidence and uncertainty rules

Signs describe a tendency under stated conditions; they do not establish a universal law. A correlation cannot supply an arrow without timing or mechanism. Do not infer loop behavior from a pretty diagram, and do not use precise coefficients, probabilities, or delay durations without provenance. If one missing link could change a reinforcing loop into a balancing loop, label the loop conflicted or held. Preserve alternative diagrams when the evidence cannot distinguish them.

## Stop conditions

Stop when the focal pattern is not repeated, a variable cannot be given an operational meaning, or the direction of an essential link is unknown. Hold the conclusion when the diagram cannot explain the observed behavior without an unsupported link. For safety-critical action, treat the loop as a hypothesis and require domain validation before intervention.

## Complement handoff

Hand off to systems-thinking when the diagram reveals missing actors, stocks, flows, boundary choices, or wider side effects. Pass the signed variables, loop types, delays, and observed pattern; systems-thinking may widen the account without treating the diagram as verified fact.
