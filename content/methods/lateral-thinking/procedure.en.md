## Purpose

Use this procedure when an obvious option set has become narrow, repetitive, or trapped by an assumption. It makes the current frame explicit, deliberately challenges or reverses selected assumptions, generates genuinely different concepts, and then sends the candidates through a separate feasibility and safety screen. The method is for opening a new solution space, not for treating novelty as evidence or skipping disciplined validation.

## Use when

Use it when a team keeps producing the same answer, optimization has reached a plateau, or the question “Could the problem be approached in a completely different way?” is central. It fits ideation for services, workflows, products, communication, and strategy when the constraints are real but the frame may be optional. Use a small set of explicit assumptions and a bounded prompt so provocation does not become an unproductive brainstorm.

## Do not use when

Do not use it as the primary method for safety-critical validation, emergency response, a binding rule, or a calculation whose correctness depends on fixed premises. Do not treat a random association or provocative phrase as a solution. Do not challenge a constraint that protects people, privacy, accessibility, law, or physical safety without an authorized alternative. If no coherent frame can be stated, hold the exercise and clarify the target.

## Inputs to establish

State the problem, current frame, non-negotiable constraints, optional assumptions, user or system affected, and decision the ideas must inform. Identify what “different” means: a new actor, timing, channel, ownership, unit, incentive, or problem definition. Ask at most two decisive questions, such as “Which assumption is a choice rather than a requirement?” or “What must every candidate still protect?”

## Procedure

1. **Describe the current frame.** Write the problem as currently understood and the option pattern it keeps producing. **Visible output/state change:** publish the frame, desired outcome, and non-negotiable constraints; mark unverified constraints separately.
2. **Extract assumptions.** List the small number of rules that make the current frame feel inevitable, then classify each as required, evidence-backed, assumed, or optional. **Visible output/state change:** publish an assumption register with its status and consequence if removed.
3. **Apply provocations.** Reverse, remove, exaggerate, relocate, split, combine, or use a bounded random stimulus on selected optional assumptions. Keep safety and legal constraints intact. **Visible output/state change:** publish the changed assumption and the new frame for each provocation.
4. **Generate distinct concepts.** Build several candidate approaches from the new frames before judging feasibility. Require each candidate to state what it changes, who benefits, and which original constraint it preserves. **Visible output/state change:** publish at least three genuinely different concepts with their originating frame.
5. **Screen and compare.** Apply a transparent safety, feasibility, user, cost, and evidence screen; reject ideas that merely rename the original option. **Visible output/state change:** publish the shortlist, rejected concepts with reasons, and unresolved checks without pretending to have precise scores.
6. **Choose a test or hand off.** Select a reversible experiment or pass the shortlist to a comparison method. State the observation that would disconfirm the leading concept and the risk boundary for trying it. **Visible output/state change:** publish the selected concept, test, owner, hold condition, and flip condition.

## Public output contract

Return the current frame, challenged assumption, provocation, at least three distinct concepts, screening rule, shortlist, and next test or handoff. Keep generation and evaluation visibly separate. The Conclusion Card should state which option is worth testing and why, not claim that it is proven; include an observable condition that would change the choice. Preserve rejected concepts only as safe, concise alternatives in the trace.

## Evidence and uncertainty rules

Ideas are generated possibilities, not evidence of demand, safety, feasibility, or effect. Label observations, constraints, inferences, and assumptions separately. Do not assign novelty, success probability, or expected impact numbers without a defensible basis. Keep a legal, accessibility, privacy, and safety screen before recommending a live test. If the leading idea depends on an untested user need or technical premise, keep it provisional and state the check.

## Stop conditions

Stop when the problem frame, protected constraints, or affected users cannot be named, when a provocation would create preventable harm, or when candidates are only cosmetic variations. Hold the recommendation when no concept survives the safety and feasibility screen or when the decisive assumption cannot be tested reversibly.

## Complement handoff

Hand off to trade-off-analysis when several safe concepts need comparison against explicit criteria and trade-offs. Pass the frame, assumptions, candidate concepts, screening results, and open checks; the complement should compare options without treating ideation as proof.
