## Purpose

Use this procedure when a problem is a pattern produced over time rather than a single isolated event. It shows how actors, components, accumulations, flows, feedback, and delays interact to create growth, decline, oscillation, or persistent resistance. The goal is a public structural account and a leverage point that could change the pattern, including side effects and the limits of the model. It is a way to widen the boundary before choosing a local fix.

## Use when

Use it when interventions are repeatedly absorbed, when a local improvement creates a problem elsewhere, when outcomes lag behind actions, or when a system exhibits recurring growth, erosion, congestion, boom-and-bust, or equilibrium. It fits organizations, services, ecosystems, markets, public programs, and product workflows where several actors or components influence one another. Qualitative diagrams are acceptable when the direction of a relationship is defensible; time-series data can strengthen the account but does not remove the need to state assumptions.

## Do not use when

Do not use this as the primary procedure for a single bounded failure with a linear, well-supported mechanism; use causal-reasoning or root-cause-analysis. Do not draw feedback loops merely because several factors are present. Avoid a large system map when the boundary, direction, or time pattern cannot be stated responsibly. Do not claim that a leverage point will work in every context, or that a diagram is a validated simulation, especially for safety-critical decisions.

## Inputs to establish

Define the behavior-over-time pattern, its time horizon, and the question the system account must answer. State the boundary, actors or components, relevant stocks or accumulations, flows that change them, measurable or observable outcomes, and likely delays. Record evidence for each directional link and note what is inferred. Ask at most two decisive questions, such as “Is this pattern repeated across more than one period?” or “Which accumulation is changing more slowly than the intervention?”

## Procedure

1. **Name the pattern and boundary.** Describe the repeated trajectory, not only the latest event, and state whose actions and which components are inside or outside the analysis. **Visible output/state change:** publish a behavior-over-time statement, time horizon, and boundary; mark a one-off pattern as unsuitable.
2. **Identify stocks and flows.** List what accumulates and the inflows or outflows that change it; distinguish a level from the rate that moves it. **Visible output/state change:** publish a stock-flow inventory with units or qualitative direction and label unmeasured elements unverified.
3. **Map interactions and feedback.** Connect variables with directional links, then trace reinforcing loops that amplify change and balancing loops that counter it. Use named variables rather than vague events. **Visible output/state change:** publish one or more signed loop descriptions and the evidence or assumption behind each link.
4. **Locate delays and side effects.** Mark where information, decisions, resources, or consequences arrive late. Check how a delayed balancing response can overshoot or how a reinforcing response can exhaust a stock. **Visible output/state change:** add delay annotations, expected side effects, and any conflict between short- and long-term results.
5. **Select a leverage point.** Compare a parameter change with changes to a feedback rule, information flow, constraint, goal, or boundary. Prefer an intervention whose mechanism and owner can be stated; do not imply a precise effect without a defensible model. **Visible output/state change:** publish the candidate intervention, targeted loop, expected direction, and possible policy resistance.
6. **Check and hold the account.** Identify the observation that would support or challenge the loop explanation and specify a safe, reversible next check when possible. **Visible output/state change:** publish a supported, provisional, or held structural conclusion with a flip condition and the unmodeled factor that matters most.

## Public output contract

Return the behavior-over-time pattern, system boundary, actors or components, stocks and flows, feedback loops, delays, leverage point, and likely side effects. Distinguish verified observations from inferred links and assumptions. Describe reinforcing and balancing loops in readable directional language, and state whether the intervention targets a parameter, rule, information flow, goal, or boundary. The Conclusion Card should carry the structural conclusion and an observable flip condition; the trace may hold the full map.

## Evidence and uncertainty rules

An arrow is a hypothesis about directional influence, not proof of causation. A loop is supported only when each material link has an observation, a defensible mechanism, or an explicitly marked assumption. Avoid fabricated stock levels, rates, delay durations, or sensitivity claims. If multiple loop structures explain the same pattern, show the alternatives and keep the conclusion provisional. A diagram may guide inquiry even when it cannot predict a numeric outcome; do not use visual complexity as evidence of precision.

## Stop conditions

Stop and redirect when the issue is a one-time event, the pattern and time horizon cannot be established, or the direction of essential links is unknowable from available evidence. Hold the conclusion when a key delay, actor, or feedback path is missing and could reverse the intervention. In safety-critical settings, treat the map as a hypothesis and require domain review before acting.

## Complement handoff

Hand off to causal-loop-diagramming when a compact signed diagram is needed for the named variables and loop types. Pass the behavior pattern, boundary, candidate variables, delays, and evidence states; the complement should refine the diagram without importing an unsupported full system narrative.
